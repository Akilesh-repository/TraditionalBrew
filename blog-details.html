The Art of
Koji Making.

Master Toji Hiroshi

Published on May 7, 2026 • 5 min read

Explore the intricate process of cultivating koji mold, the heartbeat of sake brewing. Discover how this ancient technique transforms simple rice into complex, aromatic masterpieces.

Read Article Back to Blog

The Heart of Sake: Understanding Koji

To truly understand the soul of a beautifully crafted sake, one must first understand the magic of Aspergillus oryzae, more commonly known in the brewing world as Koji-kin. Unlike wine, which relies on the naturally occurring sugars found in grapes, sake is born from rice—a grain composed primarily of dense, unfermentable starches. The yeast responsible for creating alcohol cannot process these starches directly. This is where Koji steps in, acting as the indispensable bridge between a humble grain of rice and a glass of premium sake. By carefully cultivating this specialized mold onto steamed rice, brewers initiate an enzymatic reaction that meticulously breaks down the complex starches into simple, fermentable sugars.

The process is incredibly delicate. If the mold propagates too quickly or deeply into the rice grain, the resulting sugars will be overwhelmingly abundant, leading to a cloying, heavy flavor profile that lacks nuance. Conversely, if the growth is too superficial, the yeast will starve, and the fermentation will stall entirely. For centuries, our brewers have operated under the adage "Ichi-koji, ni-moto, san-tsukuri" (First koji, second yeast starter, third the main mash). This profound statement underscores the sheer hierarchy of importance in our craft. Without flawless koji, the subsequent steps—no matter how skillfully executed—cannot salvage the brew. It sets the foundational trajectory for the aroma, acidity, and umami that will eventually define the final liquid.

The Sanctity of the Muro

Deep within the architecture of our 130-year-old brewery lies the Koji Muro—the specialized cedar-lined incubation room where the transformation takes place. Stepping into the Muro is akin to entering a sacred temple. The air is thick, profoundly warm, and intensely humid, rigorously maintained at temperatures exceeding 30 degrees Celsius (86 degrees Fahrenheit) regardless of the bitter winter chill howling outside the brewery walls. The walls are constructed from thick, untreated Japanese cedar, a wood chosen not only for its natural insulative properties but also for its ability to regulate moisture within the environment perfectly.

Working in the Muro is a testament to human endurance and passion. The kurabito (brewery workers) must continuously monitor the temperature of the rice bed, often waking every two to three hours throughout the night to break up the rice clumps by hand. This grueling physical labor, known as te-ire, prevents the core temperature of the growing mold from spiking too high, which would effectively kill the precious enzymes. Stripped down to light cotton garments, sweating profusely in the tropical heat of the room, our team relies entirely on their senses. Thermometers and modern sensors are present, of course, but true mastery is found in the way the rice feels against the palm of the hand—a tactile dialogue between the brewer and the living organism they are trying to nurture.

A Symphony of the Senses

As the 48-hour incubation period progresses, the Muro begins to undergo a magnificent sensory evolution. What starts as the simple, starchy scent of freshly steamed rice slowly morphs into something entirely different and intensely captivating. Around the 24-hour mark, a distinctly sweet, almost milky aroma begins to fill the room. By the time the koji is nearing completion, the air becomes saturated with a deep, intoxicating fragrance that master brewers lovingly compare to roasted chestnuts, warm vanilla, and sweet damp earth. It is an olfactory indicator that the enzymes have successfully synthesized and are ready to be introduced to the yeast.

Visually, the transformation is equally striking. The translucent, wet, steamed rice grains gradually become frosted with a fine, powdery white mycelium. A perfect batch of koji, known as tsuki-haze, will exhibit tiny white spots of mold that have penetrated deep into the heart of the grain while leaving the exterior relatively clean. This specific style of growth is paramount for brewing our premium Daiginjo sakes, as it ensures a slow, sustained release of glucose during the cold fermentation process, allowing the yeast to produce the brilliant, floral, and fruity esters (like melon, apple, and anise) that define our most celebrated bottles. Running a hand through a freshly finished bed of koji feels light, dry, and distinctly bouncy—a stark contrast to the sticky, heavy rice that was brought into the room two days prior.

Shaping the Final Masterpiece

When the koji is finally deemed ready, it is immediately rushed out of the sweltering Muro and spread thinly across large tables in a cool room to halt the mold's growth. This critical step locks in the exact enzymatic power the Toji desires. From here, the koji will be introduced into the fermentation vats alongside water, yeast, and additional steamed rice. Because sake undergoes a unique "multiple parallel fermentation"—where the koji breaks down starches into sugar at the exact same time the yeast converts that sugar into alcohol—the quality and enzymatic strength of the koji dictate the entire tempo of the brew.

If we are brewing a robust, earthy Tokubetsu Junmai designed to be served warm alongside grilled winter fare, we might cultivate a different style of koji—one that promotes a richer, fuller body with higher umami and amino acid content. However, for our limited-reserve Junmai Daiginjo, precision is absolute. The koji must be impossibly refined to yield a sake that dances weightlessly across the palate with crystalline purity. Every bottle of TraditionalBrew that you open, every subtle note of peach or whisper of savory rice you taste, is the direct descendant of those sleepless, sweat-drenched nights in the cedar-lined Muro. It is a living, breathing testament to an art form that refuses to be hurried, where nature and human hands work in perfect, agonizingly slow harmony.
